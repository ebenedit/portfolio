Digital Wallet. Building a product. Growing a team.

We designed a wallet for Latin America, from product definition to beta testing. Along the way, I built and led an international design team to bring it to life.

My role:
Product Design Manager
Product:
B2C wallet · Payments on merchant sites · Admin back office
Scope:
Product definition · UX/UI · Hiring · Design leadership · Design system
Markets:
Brazil, Mexico, Colombia, Argentina, Peru, Chile and Ecuador

01. The challenge

Moving your money should be easy

The challenge was to design a wallet from scratch: simple onboarding and a clear experience for adding, sending and withdrawing money, managing cards and exchanging currencies. The proposition also included loyalty and VIP programmes to encourage repeat use. Everything had to work as one product, adapting to the requirements and habits of seven markets.

I started as the only designer. I benchmarked other wallets and worked with Business to define the MVP and the rules behind the experience: how people would register, when they would verify their identity, which accounts they would have and how transaction limits would work.

With those decisions in place, I designed the first wireframes and gave the team a foundation to explore and build on.

02. Building the team

Finding the talent we needed

As the product took shape, I defined the roles, tools and budget we needed to develop it.

I led hiring: interviewing candidates, assessing their design judgement and finding a mix of skills suited to the work ahead. I hired three designers and a researcher across Spain, Argentina and Bulgaria. Building an international team also meant establishing a shared direction, clear responsibilities and a way of working together.

As people joined, I moved from designing the first flows to supporting the team, reviewing proposals and giving each person room to own their solutions.

03. Leading the work

Giving the team autonomy and focus

The product had several product owners, with features and deadlines competing for the team’s capacity. I pushed for joint planning to make priorities visible and anticipate conflicts. Before new work started, I reviewed requirements and helped clarify the problem we needed to solve.

In design reviews, my role was to challenge proposals, offer guidance and maintain consistency across flows. I wanted the team to explore independently and make decisions with a clear direction.

I also represented Design in conversations with leadership, making the team’s needs and the implications of scope and timeline decisions visible.

04. Designing the critical journeys

Building trust from the first step

Onboarding brought together one of the product’s biggest tensions: we wanted to make access easy, but we needed to collect personal information and verify users’ identities. Part of the process relied on a third-party provider, with limited scope for customisation, and requirements varied by country. We iterated through several versions to organise the steps and help users understand what they needed to do and why.

Payments on merchant sites introduced another scenario: some people would discover the wallet while trying to make a deposit. Registration became part of a task they had already started.

We designed that journey with particular attention to continuity between choosing the wallet, completing verification and making the payment.

05. Designing as a system

A shared foundation for a connected experience

We started with a visual identity created by an external agency. I brought a product perspective to the reviews and guided its application across the digital experience.

Within the team, we developed the app’s design system: colours, typography, components, illustrations and animations.

This foundation helped us maintain consistency as we worked across different features. We also reviewed the app at larger text sizes to identify where layouts needed to adapt.

06. Testing and iterating

Trust is in the details

With our researcher, we ran prototype tests and beta studies in Chile, Peru and Argentina. The findings helped us challenge decisions and identify concrete improvements.

Onboarding asked too much.
Participants compared it with local wallets they already knew. Proof of address and questions about the source of funds created friction. Even the order of document options mattered: people expected to find their national ID before their passport.

Success appeared too soon.
The screen said “Money sent!” before the transfer had finished. We replaced it with “Transfer in progress” and made the waiting time visible to reflect the actual transaction status.

Everyday actions needed more context.
We clarified fees and added descriptions to Deposit, Withdraw and Send. Later tests also highlighted issues with time estimates, transitions to third-party screens and the reasons to use the virtual card.

In the Chile and Peru beta alone, we identified 30 usability issues and 27 bugs, giving us concrete findings to guide further improvements.

07. Outcome and learning

Reaching beta and reflecting on the journey

We took an initial proposition to a product tested in three markets. Along the way, I built the team and established how we defined, reviewed and validated the experience.

The project closed before commercial launch, so we could not measure adoption or business impact at scale. My reading is that the window of opportunity had narrowed: regulations had changed and competitors were already established.

The lesson I took forward was to revisit market assumptions at every major milestone. As we validate how an experience works, we also need to check that the proposition remains relevant.

This project shows how I connect product and leadership: bringing structure to ambiguity, hiring the talent we need and helping the team turn complex decisions into clear experiences.
